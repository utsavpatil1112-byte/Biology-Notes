14.1 RESPIRATORY ORGANS & HUMAN RESPIRATORY SYSTEM NCERT Pages 183–185

Biological Concept of Respiration / Breathing

Organisms utilize oxygen (O2) to indirectly break down simple nutrient molecules like glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids to derive cellular energy for metabolic activities. Harmful carbon dioxide (CO2) released during these catabolic reactions must be removed. Breathing (Respiration) is the process of continuous exchange of atmospheric O2 with cellular CO2.

Comparative Respiratory Mechanisms Across Animal Phyla

Animal Group / Organisms Respiratory Mechanism / Structure Type of Respiration Habitat / Special Features
Lower Invertebrates (Sponges, Coelenterates, Flatworms) Simple diffusion over entire body surface Cutaneous / Surface Diffusion Aquatic or moist environments; no specialized respiratory organs
Earthworms Moist cuticle Cutaneous Respiration Moist skin facilitates direct diffusion into capillaries
Insects Network of tracheal tubes Tracheal Respiration Air tubes transport atmospheric air directly throughout the body
Aquatic Arthropods & Molluscs Specialized vascularised structures called Gills Branchial Respiration Extract dissolved oxygen from aquatic surroundings
Fishes Gills Branchial Respiration Vertebrates adapted to aquatic respiration
Amphibians (e.g., Frogs) Moist skin (Cutaneous) and Lungs (Pulmonary) Dual (Cutaneous + Pulmonary) Skin used in water/hibernation; lungs used on land
Reptiles, Birds, Mammals Vascularised bags called Lungs Pulmonary Respiration Terrestrial vertebrates with highly developed respiratory systems
🧠 Easy Way to Remember Animal Respiratory Types:

C-T-B-P: Cutaneous (Skin - Frog/Worm) ➔ Tracheal (Tubes - Insects) ➔ Branchial (Gills - Fish/Molluscs) ➔ Pulmonary (Lungs - Mammals/Birds/Reptiles).

Sequential Pathway of Human Respiratory Tract

  1. 1. External Nostrils: Pair of openings situated above the upper lips leading into the nasal passage.
  2. 2. Nasal Chamber: Passage lined with mucous membrane to filter, warm, and humidify incoming air.
  3. 3. Pharynx: Common chamber/passage for both food and atmospheric air.
  4. 4. Larynx & Glottis: Cartilaginous box assisting sound production (Sound Box). During swallowing, the glottis is covered by a thin elastic cartilaginous flap called the Epiglottis to prevent food entry.
  5. 5. Trachea: Straight tube extending to the mid-thoracic cavity. Divides at the level of the 5th thoracic vertebra into right and left primary bronchi.
  6. 6. Bronchial Tree: Primary bronchi ➔ Secondary bronchi ➔ Tertiary bronchi ➔ Initial bronchioles ➔ Terminal bronchioles.
  7. 7. Alveoli: Thin, irregular-walled, highly vascularised bag-like structures arising from terminal bronchioles.
Figure 14.1 — Schematic View of Human Respiratory System

Cartilaginous Rings support: Trachea, primary, secondary, and tertiary bronchi, and initial bronchioles to prevent collapse.

1. Conducting Part

Extent: External nostrils up to terminal bronchioles.

Functions:

  • Transports atmospheric air to the alveoli
  • Clears air from foreign particles/dust
  • Humidifies incoming air
  • Brings air temperature to body temperature
2. Respiratory / Exchange Part

Extent: Alveoli and their ducts.

Function:

  • Site of actual diffusion of $O_2$ and $CO_2$ between atmospheric air and blood
  • Extremely thin wall for rapid gas diffusion
Double Pleural Membrane Protection

Lungs are enclosed by double-layered Pleura containing pleural fluid that reduces friction on the lung surface.

  • • Outer Pleural Membrane: In close contact with the thoracic lining.
  • • Inner Pleural Membrane: In direct contact with the lung surface.
Anatomically Air-Tight Thoracic Chamber
  • • Dorsally: Vertebral column
  • • Ventrally: Sternum
  • • Laterally: Ribs
  • • Lower Side: Dome-shaped Diaphragm

*Anatomy ensures any change in thoracic volume is directly reflected in pulmonary volume (essential for breathing as we cannot alter pulmonary volume directly).

5 Fundamental Steps Involved in Respiration

Step 1 Breathing / Pulmonary Ventilation (Inflow of atmospheric O2 & outflow of CO2-rich alveolar air)
Step 2 Gas Diffusion across Alveolar Membrane
Step 3 Gas Transport by Blood
Step 4 Gas Diffusion between Blood & Tissues
Step 5 Cellular O2 Utilisation & CO2 Release
14.2 MECHANISM OF BREATHING NCERT Pages 185–186

Pressure Gradient Rule of Breathing

Air movement into and out of lungs is driven by a pressure gradient between lungs and atmosphere. Inspiration occurs when intra-pulmonary pressure is lower than atmospheric pressure (negative pressure). Expiration occurs when intra-pulmonary pressure is higher than atmospheric pressure.

Detailed Mechanical Comparison: Inspiration vs Expiration

Parameter Inspiration (Inhalation) Expiration (Exhalation)
Definition Process by which atmospheric air is drawn into the lungs. Process by which alveolar air is expelled out of the lungs.
Pressure State Intra-pulmonary pressure < Atmospheric pressure (Negative intra-pulmonary pressure). Intra-pulmonary pressure > Atmospheric pressure (Positive intra-pulmonary pressure).
Diaphragm Action Contracts and moves downwards (flattens), increasing thoracic volume in Antero-posterior axis. Relaxes and arches upwards to dome shape, decreasing thoracic volume in antero-posterior axis.
Intercostal Muscles External intercostal muscles contract, lifting ribs and sternum. External intercostals relax; sternum and ribs return to original position.
Thoracic Axis Expansion Increases thoracic volume in Dorso-ventral axis. Reduces thoracic volume in dorso-ventral axis.
Pulmonary Volume & Pressure Pulmonary volume increases ➔ Intra-pulmonary pressure decreases below atmospheric ➔ Air rushes IN. Pulmonary volume decreases ➔ Intra-pulmonary pressure increases above atmospheric ➔ Air forced OUT.
⭐ Abdominal Muscles

Humans possess the ability to increase the strength of inspiration and expiration using additional muscles in the abdomen.

⭐ Normal Breathing Rate

A healthy adult human breathes on average 12 – 16 times per minute under physiological rest conditions.

⭐ Clinical Spirometer

The volume of air involved in breathing movements is measured using a Spirometer for clinical assessment of pulmonary functions.

(b) EXPIRATION

14.2.1 RESPIRATORY VOLUMES AND CAPACITIES NCERT Pages 186–187

Clinical Importance of Volumes & Capacities

Measurement of respiratory volumes and derivation of pulmonary capacities are essential diagnostic tools in clinical medicine for evaluating pulmonary health, lung compliance, and respiratory disorders.

1. Respiratory Volumes (Measured Directly by Spirometer)

Volume Name Abbreviation Average Volume Value Complete NCERT Definition
Tidal Volume TV ~500 mL
(6000–8000 mL/min)		 Volume of air inspired or expired during a normal respiration. A healthy man can inspire or expire approx 6000–8000 mL per minute.
Inspiratory Reserve Volume IRV 2500 mL – 3000 mL Additional volume of air a person can inspire by a forcible inspiration.
Expiratory Reserve Volume ERV 1000 mL – 1100 mL Additional volume of air a person can expire by a forcible expiration.
Residual Volume RV 1100 mL – 1200 mL Volume of air remaining in the lungs even after a forcible expiration. (Cannot be measured directly by simple spirometer).

2. Pulmonary Capacities (Derived by Combining Volumes)

Capacity Name Abbreviation Formula Approximate Value Complete NCERT Explanation
Inspiratory Capacity IC TV + IRV ~3000 – 3500 mL Total volume of air a person can inspire after a normal expiration.
Expiratory Capacity EC TV + ERV ~1500 – 1600 mL Total volume of air a person can expire after a normal inspiration.
Functional Residual Capacity FRC ERV + RV ~2100 – 2300 mL Volume of air that remains in lungs after a normal expiration.
Vital Capacity VC ERV + TV + IRV ~3500 – 4700 mL Maximum volume of air a person can breathe in after a forced expiration (or breathe out after forced inspiration).
Total Lung Capacity TLC RV + ERV + TV + IRV
(or VC + RV)		 ~4600 – 5800 mL Total volume of air accommodated in lungs at the end of a forced inspiration.
🧠 Easy Memory Tricks for Capacities:
  • • IC = Tidal + Inspire (TV + IRV)
  • • EC = Tidal + Expire (TV + ERV)
  • • FRC = What stays after normal breath = ERV + RV
  • • VC = Total usable air = ERV + TV + IRV
  • • TLC = Absolute Maximum Air = Vital Capacity + Residual Volume
⭐ Practice Calculation Note (Hourly Tidal Volume):

If normal TV = 500 mL, and breathing rate = 12 to 16 times/min:
Per minute volume = $500 \times 12 \text{ to } 500 \times 16 = 6000 \text{ to } 8000 \text{ mL/min}$.
Hourly Volume: $6000 \times 60 \text{ to } 8000 \times 60 = \mathbf{360,000 \text{ mL (360 L) to } 480,000 \text{ mL (480 L) per hour}}$.

14.3 EXCHANGE OF GASES NCERT Pages 187–188

Primary Sites & Diffusion Factors

Alveoli are the primary sites of gas exchange. Exchange also occurs between blood and body tissues. Gas exchange occurs by simple diffusion based primarily on partial pressure gradients, solubility of gases, and thickness of diffusion membrane.

TABLE 14.1 — Partial Pressures (in mm Hg) of Oxygen and Carbon Dioxide

Respiratory Gas Atmospheric Air Alveoli Deoxygenated Blood Oxygenated Blood Body Tissues
Oxygen (O2) 159 104 40 95 40
Carbon Dioxide (CO2) 0.3 40 45 40 45

*The table shows a steep concentration gradient for O2 from alveoli (104) ➔ blood (40) ➔ tissues (40). Conversely, CO2 gradient flows from tissues (45) ➔ blood (45) ➔ alveoli (40).

⭐ Critical Fact: Solubility Difference Between Gases

The solubility of CO2 is 20 to 25 times higher than that of O2. Therefore, the amount of CO2 that can diffuse through the diffusion membrane per unit difference in partial pressure is much higher compared to that of O2.

⭐ Critical Fact: Solubility Difference Between Gases

The solubility of $CO_2$ is 20 to 25 times higher than that of $O_2$. Therefore, the amount of $CO_2$ that can diffuse through the diffusion membrane per unit difference in partial pressure is much higher compared to that of $O_2$.

The Three Major Layers of the Diffusion Membrane

Total thickness of diffusion membrane is much less than a millimeter (< 1 mm), facilitating rapid diffusion.

  1. 1. Thin Squamous Epithelium: Single-celled thick lining of the alveolar wall.
  2. 2. Endothelium of Alveolar Capillaries: Single-celled endothelial wall surrounding capillary blood vessels.
  3. 3. Basement Substance: Composed of a thin basement membrane supporting alveolar epithelium + basement membrane surrounding capillary endothelial cells.
Figure 14.4 — Diagram of Section of an Alveolus with Pulmonary Capillary

Total diffusion barrier thickness < 1 mm, creating ideal conditions for gas exchange.

14.4 TRANSPORT OF GASES NCERT Pages 189–190

Transport Mechanisms of Oxygen (O2)

  • As Oxyhaemoglobin (in RBCs): 97%
  • Dissolved State in Blood Plasma: 3%

Transport Mechanisms of Carbon Dioxide (CO2)

  • As Bicarbonate Ions (HCO3-): 70%
  • As Carbamino-haemoglobin (RBCs): 20 – 25%
  • Dissolved State in Plasma: 7%

14.4.1 Transport of Oxygen & Oxygen Dissociation Curve

Haemoglobin is a red-coloured iron-containing pigment present in RBCs. O2 binds reversibly with haemoglobin to form Oxyhaemoglobin. Each haemoglobin molecule can carry a maximum of 4 molecules of O2.

Oxygen Dissociation Curve Definition

A Sigmoid curve obtained when percentage saturation of haemoglobin with O2 is plotted against partial pressure of oxygen (pO2).

Conditions Favouring Binding in Alveoli (Shift to Left):
  • High pO2
  • Low pCO2
  • Lesser H+ ion concentration (higher pH)
  • Lower temperature
Conditions Favouring Dissociation in Tissues (Shift to Right):
  • Low pO2
  • High pCO2
  • High H+ ion concentration (lower pH)
  • Higher temperature
⭐ NCERT Delivery Value:

Every 100 mL of oxygenated blood delivers approximately 5 mL of O2 to the tissues under normal physiological conditions.

Figure 14.5 — Oxygen Dissociation Curve (Sigmoid Graph)

Sigmoid shape reflects cooperative binding of oxygen to 4 haemoglobin subunits.

14.4.2 Transport of Carbon Dioxide & Carbonic Anhydrase Reaction

CO2 is carried by haemoglobin as Carbamino-haemoglobin (20–25%). Binding is governed by pCO2. High pCO2 and low pO2 in tissues promote binding; low pCO2 and high pO2 in alveoli promote dissociation.

Reversible Enzymatic Reaction (Carbonic Anhydrase)

RBCs contain an extremely high concentration of the enzyme Carbonic Anhydrase (minute quantities also exist in plasma).

CO2 + H2O ⇌ (Carbonic Anhydrase) ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ (Carbonic Anhydrase) ⇌ HCO3- + H+
At Tissue Site (High pCO2): CO2 diffuses into blood and forms HCO3- and H+.
At Alveolar Site (Low pCO2): Reaction proceeds in reverse direction, converting HCO3- back to CO2 and H2O to be expired.
⭐ NCERT Delivery Value for CO2:

Every 100 mL of deoxygenated blood delivers approximately 4 mL of CO2 to the alveoli.

14.5 REGULATION OF RESPIRATION & 14.6 DISORDERS NCERT Pages 190–191

14.5 Neural & Chemical Control of Respiration

1. Respiratory Rhythm Centre

Located in the Medulla oblongata region of brain. Primary centre responsible for maintaining basic respiratory rhythm.

2. Pneumotaxic Centre

Located in the Pons region of brain. Moderates functions of rhythm centre. Signals reduce duration of inspiration, altering respiratory rate.

3. Chemosensitive Area

Situated adjacent to rhythm centre. Highly sensitive to CO2 and H+ ions. Activates rhythm centre to speed up elimination when levels rise.

4. Aortic Arch & Carotid Receptors

Receptors associated with aortic arch and carotid artery recognize CO2 and H+ concentration changes and send signals to rhythm centre.

⭐ Crucial NCERT Exception Rule:

The role of Oxygen (O2) in the regulation of respiratory rhythm is quite insignificant. Regulation is primarily driven by CO2 and H+ ion levels.

14.6 Disorders of Respiratory System

1. Asthma

Difficulty in breathing causing wheezing due to inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles.

2. Emphysema

A chronic disorder in which alveolar walls are damaged, resulting in a permanent decrease in respiratory surface area. Major cause: Cigarette smoking.

3. Occupational Disorders

Seen in industries involving grinding or stone-breaking. Excessive dust causes inflammation leading to Fibrosis (proliferation of fibrous tissues) and serious lung damage. Protective masks are essential.

📝 NCERT CHAPTER EXERCISES & DETAILED SOLUTIONS Page 192 • Questions 1 to 14
Q1. Define vital capacity. What is its significance?

Answer: Vital Capacity (VC) is defined as the maximum volume of air a person can breathe in after a forced expiration (or the maximum volume of air a person can breathe out after a forced inspiration). Formula: VC = ERV + TV + IRV.
Significance: It indicates the maximum working capacity and strength of the respiratory muscles and lungs. Higher vital capacity provides greater ability to supply O2 during intense muscular exercise.

Q2. State the volume of air remaining in the lungs after a normal breathing.

Answer: The volume of air remaining in lungs after normal expiration is called Functional Residual Capacity (FRC).
FRC = ERV + RV = 1000–1100 mL + 1100–1200 mL = 2100 to 2300 mL.

Q3. Diffusion of gases occurs in the alveolar region only and not in the other parts of respiratory system. Why?

Answer: Diffusion occurs exclusively in alveoli because alveoli possess an extremely thin diffusion membrane (< 1 mm) made of single-celled squamous epithelium closely lined with blood capillaries, providing a huge surface area. Other conducting parts (trachea, bronchi) have thick cartilaginous rings and ciliated epithelium meant only for air transport.

Q4. What are the major transport mechanisms for CO2? Explain.

Answer: CO2 is transported by 3 mechanisms:
1. As Bicarbonate Ions (70%): CO2 reacts with H2O inside RBCs catalyzed by Carbonic Anhydrase to form H2CO3, which dissociates into HCO3- and H+.
2. As Carbamino-haemoglobin (20–25%): CO2 binds directly to the amino groups of haemoglobin in RBCs.
3. Dissolved in Plasma (7%): Transported in physical solution state in blood plasma.

Q5. What will be the pO2 and pCO2 in the atmospheric air compared to those in the alveolar air?

Answer: Correct Option: (ii) pO2 higher, pCO2 lesser.
Explanation: Atmospheric air has pO2 = 159 mm Hg and pCO2 = 0.3 mm Hg, whereas alveolar air has pO2 = 104 mm Hg and pCO2 = 40 mm Hg.

Q6. Explain the process of inspiration under normal conditions.

Answer: Inspiration is an active process initiated by: (1) Contraction of diaphragm flattening it and increasing thoracic volume in antero-posterior axis. (2) Contraction of external intercostal muscles lifting ribs and sternum, increasing volume in dorso-ventral axis. The overall increase in thoracic volume increases pulmonary volume, dropping intra-pulmonary pressure below atmospheric pressure, forcing atmospheric air into lungs.

Q7. How is respiration regulated?

Answer: Respiration is regulated neurally and chemically: (1) Respiratory Rhythm Centre in Medulla maintains basic rhythm. (2) Pneumotaxic Centre in Pons reduces inspiration duration. (3) Chemosensitive Area adjacent to rhythm centre responds to elevated CO2 and H+ ions. (4) Receptors in Aortic arch & Carotid artery detect CO2 / H+ changes and send corrective signals.

Q8. What is the effect of pCO2 on oxygen transport?

Answer: High pCO2 lowers haemoglobin affinity for O2 (Bohr Effect), shifting oxygen dissociation curve to the right and promoting O2 release to tissues. Conversely, low pCO2 in alveoli promotes O2 binding to haemoglobin.

Q9. What happens to the respiratory process in a man going up a hill?

Answer: At high altitudes, atmospheric pressure and pO2 decrease, leading to hypoxia. To compensate, breathing rate increases, heart rate increases, and over time RBC production increases (stimulated by erythropoietin).

Q10. What is the site of gaseous exchange in an insect?

Answer: Tracheoles (the finest branches of the tracheal network) where direct gas exchange occurs with body tissues.

Q11. Define oxygen dissociation curve. Can you suggest any reason for its sigmoidal pattern?

Answer: It is the graphic plot of percentage saturation of haemoglobin against partial pressure of oxygen (pO2).
Reason for Sigmoidal Pattern: Cooperative binding of oxygen. Binding of the first O2 molecule to one haem subunit induces a conformational change that increases affinity of remaining subunits for O2.

Q12. Have you heard about hypoxia? Try to gather information about it, and discuss with your friends.

Answer: Hypoxia is a pathological condition where body tissues receive insufficient oxygen supply. Causes include high altitude (hypoxic hypoxia), anaemia (anaemic hypoxia), or toxic chemicals like carbon monoxide poisoning.

Q13. Distinguish between: (a) IRV and ERV (b) Inspiratory capacity and Expiratory capacity (c) Vital capacity and Total lung capacity.

Answer:
(a) IRV vs ERV: IRV is extra volume inspired forcibly (~2500–3000 mL); ERV is extra volume expired forcibly (~1000–1100 mL).
(b) IC vs EC: IC = TV + IRV (~3000–3500 mL); EC = TV + ERV (~1500–1600 mL).
(c) VC vs TLC: VC = ERV + TV + IRV (~3500–4700 mL); TLC = VC + RV (~4600–5800 mL).

Q14. What is Tidal volume? Find out the Tidal volume (approximate value) for a healthy human in an hour.

Answer: Tidal Volume (TV) is the volume of air inspired or expired during a normal breath (~500 mL).
Hourly Calculation:
Per minute volume = 500 mL × 12 to 16 breaths/min = 6000 to 8000 mL/min.
Per hour volume = 6000 to 8000 mL × 60 minutes = 360,000 mL (360 L) to 480,000 mL (480 L).
