17 17.1 TYPES OF MOVEMENT

NCERT Pages 217–218

Movement vs. Locomotion

Movement is a primary characteristic feature of all living organisms, ranging from unicellular streaming of protoplasm in Amoeba to movement of cilia, flagella, tentacles, and limbs. When voluntary movements result in a change of place or location, it is termed Locomotion (e.g., walking, running, climbing, flying, swimming).

🔑 Core Biological Golden Rule:

"All locomotions are movements, but all movements are not locomotions."

Ecological Significance of Locomotion:

Animals locomote in search of food, shelter, mate, suitable breeding grounds, favourable climatic conditions, or to escape from predators/enemies.

Three Main Types of Body Cell Movements

1. Amoeboid Movement:

Exhibited by specialized cells like macrophages and leucocytes in human blood. Effected by pseudopodia formed by protoplasmic streaming. Cytoskeletal elements like microfilaments are actively involved.

2. Ciliary Movement:

Occurs in internal tubular organs lined by ciliated epithelium. Coordinated ciliary sweeps in the trachea clear dust and foreign particles. Also facilitates passage of ova through the female reproductive tract (fallopian tubes).

3. Muscular Movement:

Required for movements of limbs, jaws, tongue, etc. Relies on the contractile properties of muscles. Requires perfect coordination between muscular, skeletal, and neural systems.

Detailed Anatomy of a Skeletal Muscle Bundle

  • • Fascicles: Each skeletal muscle is composed of multiple muscle bundles called fascicles.
  • • Fascia: Fascicles are held together by a common collagenous connective tissue layer named fascia.
  • • Muscle Fibre (Cell): Each fascicle contains numerous muscle fibres. The cell membrane is the Sarcolemma, enclosing Sarcoplasm.
  • • Syncytium: Muscle fibre is a syncytium because sarcoplasm contains multiple nuclei.
  • • Sarcoplasmic Reticulum: Specialized endoplasmic reticulum acting as the primary storehouse of Calcium ions ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$).
  • • Myofibrils / Myofilaments: Parallelly arranged filaments in sarcoplasm exhibiting alternate dark (A-band) and light (I-band).

Sarcomere Functional Regions & Formulas

• I-Band (Isotropic Band): Light band containing only Actin (thin) filaments. Bisected by elastic fibre called the Z-line.

• A-Band (Anisotropic Band): Dark band containing Myosin (thick) filaments along with overlapping thin filaments. Held in middle by thin fibrous M-line.

• H-Zone: Central part of thick filament not overlapped by thin filaments in resting state.

• Sarcomere Definition: The portion of myofibril between two successive Z-lines; considered the functional unit of contraction.

📐 Sarcomere Structural Mathematical Formula:

$$1 \text{ Sarcomere} = \frac{1}{2} (\text{I-Band}) + 1 \text{ Full A-Band} + \frac{1}{2} (\text{I-Band})$$ $$\text{Resting H-Zone} = \text{Length of A-Band} - \text{Overlapping Thin Filaments}$$

17.2.1 Structure of Contractile Proteins (Actin & Myosin)

Thin Filament (Actin) Components

  • • F-Actin (Filamentous): Two 'F' actins helically wound together. Each F-actin is a polymer of monomeric 'G' (Globular) actins.
  • • Tropomyosin: Two filaments of tropomyosin run close to F-actins throughout its length.
  • • Troponin: Complex protein distributed at regular intervals on tropomyosin. In resting state, a subunit of troponin masks active binding sites for myosin on actin filaments.

Thick Filament (Myosin) Components

  • • Meromyosins: Myosin is a polymerised protein made of monomeric units called Meromyosins.
  • • Heavy Meromyosin (HMM): Consists of Globular Head + Short Arm (forms Cross Arm projecting outwards).
  • • Light Meromyosin (LMM): Constitutes the Tail portion.
  • • Active ATPase Head: Globular head has active ATPase enzyme sites, ATP binding sites, and active sites for actin.

⚡ 17.2.2 MECHANISM OF MUSCLE CONTRACTION (SLIDING FILAMENT THEORY)

NCERT Pages 221–223

Sequential Biochemical & Biophysical Steps in Muscle Contraction

1

CNS Neural Signal

Signal sent via motor neuron to Neuromuscular Junction (Motor-end plate). Releases Acetylcholine (ACh).

2

Action Potential & $\text{Ca}^{2+}$

Action potential spreads through sarcolemma. Causes release of $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ions from sarcoplasmic reticulum into sarcoplasm.

3

Unmasking Active Sites

$\text{Ca}^{2+}$ binds subunit of Troponin, removing masking from active myosin binding sites on actin filaments.

4

Cross Bridge Power Stroke

Myosin head hydrolyses ATP ($\text{ADP} + \text{P}_i$), binds actin to form Cross Bridge, pulling Z-lines inward toward center of A-band.

5

Relaxation Phase

New ATP binds, cross-bridge breaks. $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ pumped back to sarcoplasmic cisternae. Troponin re-masks actin sites.

🧪 Key Biochemical Equations of Contraction & Fatigue:

1. ATP Hydrolysis by Myosin Head:

$$\text{ATP} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{Myosin ATPase}} \text{ADP} + \text{P}_i + \text{Energy (Power Stroke)}$$

2. Anaerobic Glycogen Breakdown (Muscle Fatigue):

$$\text{Muscle Glycogen} \xrightarrow{\text{Anaerobic Glycolysis}} \text{Lactic Acid Accumulation} \implies \text{Muscle Fatigue}$$

Structural Band Alterations During Contraction

  • ✅ I-Band: Gets reduced/shortened as thin filaments slide inwards.
  • ✅ H-Zone: Reduces and completely disappears upon maximal contraction.
  • ✅ Sarcomere: Overall length shortens (Z-lines pulled together).
  • ⚠️ A-Band: RETAINS ITS ORIGINAL LENGTH (Does not shorten!).

Comparison: Red Muscle Fibres vs. White Muscle Fibres

Feature Red Fibres (Aerobic) White Fibres (Anaerobic)
Myoglobin Content Very High (Reddish appearance) Very Low (Pale or whitish appearance)
Mitochondria Plenty (Utilises $\text{O}_2$ for ATP) Few in number
Sarcoplasmic Reticulum Moderate amount Very High amount

A. Axial Skeleton Breakdown (80 Bones)

Distributed along central body axis
1. Skull (22 Bones)

• Cranial (8): Frontal, Parietal (2), Temporal (2), Occipital, Sphenoid, Ethmoid.

• Facial (14): Form front skull part (Nasal, Maxilla, Zygomatic, Mandible, etc.).

• Dicondylic Skull: Articulates with atlas vertebra via 2 Occipital Condyles.

2. Hyoid & Ear Ossicles (7 Bones)

• Hyoid Bone (1): Single U-shaped bone present at base of buccal cavity.

• Ear Ossicles ($6 = 3 \times 2$): Malleus, Incus, Stapes in middle ears.

3. Vertebral Column (26 Units)

• Cervical (7): Neck region ($\text{C}_1 - \text{C}_7$). $\text{C}_1 = \text{Atlas}$.

• Thoracic (12): $\text{T}_1 - \text{T}_{12}$.

• Lumbar (5): $\text{L}_1 - \text{L}_5$.

• Sacral (1 fused): 5 fused into 1.

• Coccygeal (1 fused): 4 fused into 1.

4. Sternum & Ribs (25 Bones)

• Sternum (1): Flat bone on ventral midline of thorax.

• True Ribs (Pairs 1–7): Dorsally attached to thoracic vertebrae, ventrally to sternum via hyaline cartilage.

• False Ribs (Pairs 8–10): Vertebrochondral ribs; join 7th rib via hyaline cartilage.

• Floating Ribs (Pairs 11–12): Not connected ventrally.

17.4 Classification of Joints

Joints are points of contact between bones, or between bones and cartilages. Force generated by muscles is used to carry out movement through joints, where the joint acts as a fulcrum.

Joint Category Structural Feature Degree of Movement Textbook Examples
1. Fibrous Joints Bones fused end-to-end with dense fibrous connective tissue No movement (Immovable) Sutures of flat skull bones forming cranium
2. Cartilaginous Joints Bones joined together with the help of cartilages Permits limited movements Joints between adjacent vertebrae in vertebral column
3. Synovial Joints Fluid-filled synovial cavity between articulating surfaces Considerable movement (Freely movable) • Ball & Socket: Humerus & Pectoral girdle
• Hinge Joint: Knee & Elbow joint
• Pivot Joint: Between Atlas ($\text{C}_1$) and Axis ($\text{C}_2$)
• Gliding Joint: Between Carpals
• Saddle Joint: Between Carpal & Metacarpal of thumb

🩺 17.5 Clinical Disorders of Muscular and Skeletal Systems

1. Myasthenia Gravis

Autoimmune disorder affecting neuromuscular junction. Leads to fatigue, weakening, and paralysis of skeletal muscle.

2. Muscular Dystrophy

Progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle, mostly due to a genetic disorder.

3. Tetany

Rapid spasms (wild contractions) in muscle due to low $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ levels in body fluid.

4. Arthritis

Inflammation of joints causing severe pain and stiffness.

5. Osteoporosis

Age-related disorder characterized by decreased bone mass and increased risk of fractures. Common cause: Decreased levels of estrogen.

6. Gout

Inflammation of joints due to accumulation of uric acid crystals.

Q1. Draw the diagram of a sarcomere of skeletal muscle showing different regions.

Answer: Refer to Figure 17.2 above in notes. Key labelled regions include: Z-line (bisects I-band), I-band (Actin thin filaments only), A-band (Myosin thick filaments), H-zone (un-overlapped central myosin zone), and M-line (central fibrous line).

Q2. Define sliding filament theory of muscle contraction.

Answer: The sliding filament theory states that contraction of a muscle fibre takes place by the sliding of the thin (actin) filaments over the thick (myosin) filaments towards the center of the A-band, resulting in the shortening of the sarcomere.

Q3. Describe the important steps in muscle contraction.

Answer: 1. CNS sends motor nerve signal $\rightarrow$ Acetylcholine release at motor end plate. 2. Action potential in sarcolemma causes $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ release from sarcoplasmic reticulum. 3. $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ binds Troponin, unmasking myosin sites on actin. 4. Myosin head hydrolyses ATP, forms cross-bridge, and pulls actin filaments inward (power stroke). 5. New ATP binds, breaking cross-bridge; $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ pumped back for relaxation.
