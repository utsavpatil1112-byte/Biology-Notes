Complete Concept Architecture
1. BODY FLUIDS
  • Plasma (55%): Water (90-92%), Proteins (6-8%: Fibrinogen, Globulin, Albumin), Minerals, Inactive factors.
  • Formed Elements (45%):
    • RBCs: 5-5.5 M/mm³, Biconcave, Enucleated, Hb (12-16g/100ml).
    • WBCs: 6000-8000/mm³ (Granulocytes vs Agranulocytes).
    • Platelets: 1.5-3.5 Lakhs/mm³, Megakaryocyte fragments.
  • Lymph: Interstitial fluid, high lymphocytes, fat absorption in lacteals.
2. BLOOD GROUPS & CLOTTING
  • ABO System: Antigens A/B on RBC, Antibodies in plasma.
    • O - Universal Donor
    • AB - Universal Recipient
  • Rh Factor: Rh+ve (80%), Rh-ve (20%).
    • Erythroblastosis Foetalis (Mother Rh-ve, Foetus Rh+ve).
  • Coagulation Cascade: Injured tissue/Platelets → Thrombokinase → Prothrombin → Thrombin → Fibrinogen → Fibrin Clot ($Ca^{2+}$ key).
3. HUMAN HEART & CARDIAC CYCLE
  • Anatomy: Mesodermal, Pericardium, 4-Chambered, Septa, Valves (Tricuspid, Bicuspid, Semilunar).
  • Nodal Tissue (Myogenic): SAN (Pacemaker 70-75/min) → AVN → AV Bundle → Purkinje Fibres.
  • Cardiac Cycle (0.8s): Joint Diastole → Atrial Systole (+30% filling) → Ventricular Systole → Ventricular Diastole.
  • Cardiac Output: SV (70ml) × HR (72) = ~5000 mL/min (5L).
4. ECG, CIRCULATION & DISORDERS
  • ECG Waves: P (Atrial Depolarisation), QRS (Ventricular Depolarisation), T (Ventricular Repolarisation).
  • Double Circulation: Pulmonary (RV → Lungs → LA) + Systemic (LV → Body → RA). Tunica Intima, Media, Externa.
  • Special Systems: Hepatic Portal System, Coronary System.
  • Disorders: Hypertension (>140/90), CAD/Atherosclerosis, Angina Pectoris, Heart Failure.
15.1

BLOOD & ITS COMPOSITION

NCERT Page 193–194

Definitive NCERT Concept

Blood is a special fluid connective tissue consisting of a fluid matrix called plasma and suspended formed elements.

15.1.1 Plasma

55% of Blood
  • Physical Nature: Straw-coloured, viscous fluid.
  • Water Content: Constitutes 90–92% of total plasma.
  • Proteins: Contribute 6–8% of plasma. Major proteins include:
    1. Fibrinogen: Essential for blood clotting / coagulation.
    2. Globulins: Primarily involved in defense mechanisms / immunity.
    3. Albumins: Maintain osmotic balance and fluid pressure.
  • Mineral Content: Small amounts of ions like $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Ca}^{++}$, $\text{Mg}^{++}$, $\text{HCO}_3^-$, $\text{Cl}^-$, etc.
  • Nutrients in Transit: Glucose, amino acids, lipids, etc., are continuously in transit.
  • Clotting Factors: Present in plasma in an inactive form.
⭐ Key Distinction (Serum):
$\text{Serum} = \text{Plasma} - \text{Clotting Factors}$

Blood Composition at a Glance

PLASMA (55%) Liquid Matrix
Water: 90-92% Proteins: 6-8% Ions & Nutrients: ~1-2%
FORMED ELEMENTS (45%) Cellular Components
Erythrocytes (RBC) Leucocytes (WBC) Thrombocytes (Platelets)
NCERT Exam Note:

Formed elements constitute 45% of blood volume. Plasma constitutes 55%. All blood cells originate in the bone marrow, with erythrocytes being the most abundant.

15.1.2

FORMED ELEMENTS (RBC, WBC, PLATELETS)

NCERT Page 194–195

Erythrocytes (RBCs)

Red Blood Cells
  • Count: Most abundant cells; 5.0 to 5.5 million $\text{mm}^{-3}$ of blood in healthy adult male.
  • Site of Formation: Formed in red bone marrow in adults.
  • Structure & Shape: Biconcave shape; devoid of nucleus (enucleated) in most mammals.
  • Haemoglobin: Red-coloured, iron-containing complex protein.
    • Normal Level: 12–16 gms per 100 mL of blood.
    • Function: Crucial role in transport of respiratory gases ($O_2$ and $CO_2$).
  • Life Span & Destruction: Average life span of 120 days. Destroyed in the spleen (known as the graveyard of RBCs).

Leucocytes (WBCs)

White Blood Cells
  • Color & Nucleus: Colourless due to lack of haemoglobin; distinctly nucleated.
  • Count: Relatively lesser in number; averages 6,000–8,000 $\text{mm}^{-3}$ of blood.
  • Life Span: Generally short-lived.
  • Categories: Divided into Granulocytes and Agranulocytes.
  • Immune Function: Primary defense system against foreign pathogens and allergens.

Thrombocytes (Platelets)

Cell Fragments
  • Origin: Cell fragments produced from megakaryocytes (special large cells in bone marrow).
  • Normal Count: 1,50,000 to 3,50,000 $\text{mm}^{-3}$ of blood.
  • Function: Release substances involved in coagulation or clotting of blood.
  • Clinical Significance: Reduction in platelet count leads to clotting disorders causing excessive blood loss from the body.

Detailed Classification & Comparison of Leucocytes (WBCs)

  • Multi-lobed nucleus
  • Phagocytic cells
  • Bilobed nucleus
  • Stain with acidic dyes (Eosin)
  • S-shaped nucleus
  • Stain with basic dyes
  • Kidney/Bean-shaped nucleus
  • Large motility
  • Large spherical nucleus
  • Two major forms: B & T Lymphocytes

    BLOOD GROUPS (ABO & Rh SYSTEM)

    NCERT Page 195–196

    15.1.3.1 ABO Grouping

    ABO grouping is based on the presence or absence of two surface antigens (chemicals that induce immune response) on the RBCs, namely A and B.

    Similarly, the plasma of different individuals contains two natural antibodies (proteins produced in response to antigens), anti-A and anti-B.

    NCERT TABLE 15.1: Blood Groups and Donor Compatibility
    Blood Group Antigens on RBCs Antibodies in Plasma Donor's Group
    A A anti-B A, O
    B B anti-A B, O
    AB A, B nil AB, A, B, O
    O nil anti-A, B O
    Universal Donor: Group 'O' Group O blood lacks A and B surface antigens, so it can be donated to persons with any other blood group.
    Universal Recipient: Group 'AB' Group AB individuals lack antibodies against A and B in plasma, so they can accept blood from any group.

    15.1.3.2 Rh Grouping & Clinical Case

    Rh Antigen: Similar to antigen in Rhesus monkeys; present on RBC surface of nearly 80% of humans ($\text{Rh}^{+\text{ve}}$). Individuals without it are ($\text{Rh}^{-\text{ve}}$).

    If an $\text{Rh}^{-\text{ve}}$ person is exposed to $\text{Rh}^{+\text{ve}}$ blood, specific anti-Rh antibodies form in their plasma.

    ⚠️ ERYTHROBLASTOSIS FOETALIS (Rh Incompatibility) High Yield Exam Case
    • Condition setup: $\text{Rh}^{-\text{ve}}$ pregnant mother carrying an $\text{Rh}^{+\text{ve}}$ foetus.
    • First Pregnancy: Rh antigens do not expose during gestation due to separation by the placenta. However, during delivery, maternal blood can be exposed to small amounts of $\text{Rh}^{+\text{ve}}$ foetal blood.
    • Sensitisation: Mother starts producing anti-Rh antibodies in her blood.
    • Subsequent Pregnancy ($\text{Rh}^{+\text{ve}}$ foetus): Maternal anti-Rh antibodies cross the placenta into the foetus and destroy foetal RBCs.
    • Consequences: Severe anaemia and jaundice to the newborn; can be fatal.
    • Prevention / Medical Cure: Administering anti-Rh antibodies (RhoGAM) to the mother immediately after delivery of the first child.
    15.1.4

    MECHANISM OF BLOOD COAGULATION (CLOTTING)

    NCERT Page 196

    Blood exhibits coagulation in response to injury or trauma to prevent excessive blood loss. Over time, a dark reddish-brown scum forms at the cut site.

    The Clot / Coagulum: Consists mainly of a network of threads called fibrins in which dead and damaged formed elements of blood are trapped.

    Key Highlights & Role of Ions:
    • Cascade Process: A series of linked enzymic reactions involving plasma factors present in inactive state.
    • Stimulus: Injury stimulates blood platelets AND injured tissue to release clotting factors.
    • Essential Mineral: Calcium ions ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) play an indispensable, critical role in blood clotting.
    ⚡ THE ENZYMATIC CASCADE OF BLOOD CLOTTING
    Injury / Trauma → Platelets & Tissue release Clotting Factors
    ↓
    Formation of Enzyme Complex: THROMBOKINASE
    ↓ (Requires $\text{Ca}^{2+}$)
    Inactive Prothrombin ── Thrombokinase ──► Active THROMBIN
    ↓
    Inactive Fibrinogen ── Thrombin ──► Insoluble FIBRIN THREADS
    ↓
    Fibrins + Trapped Damaged Formed Elements = CLOT / COAGULUM
    15.2

    LYMPH (TISSUE FLUID)

    NCERT Page 197

    Formation of Interstitial / Tissue Fluid

    As blood passes through tissue capillaries, water along with small water-soluble substances moves out into intercellular spaces, leaving behind larger proteins and formed elements inside blood vessels.

    This released fluid is called interstitial fluid or tissue fluid. It has the same mineral distribution as plasma.

    An elaborate network of vessels called the lymphatic system collects tissue fluid and drains it back into major veins. The fluid in the lymphatic system is called lymph.

    NCERT Comparison: Blood vs Lymph

    Property Blood Lymph (Tissue Fluid)
    Color Red (due to Haemoglobin) Colourless
    Formed Elements RBCs, WBCs, Platelets No RBCs; rich in specialised Lymphocytes
    Protein Content High protein content (6–8%) Fewer proteins
    Key Functions O2/CO2 transport, clotting Immune response, carries nutrients/hormones, fat absorption in lacteals (intestinal villi)
    15.3

    CIRCULATORY PATHWAYS & EVOLUTION OF VERTEBRATE HEART

    NCERT Page 197

    1. Open vs Closed Circulatory Systems

    Feature Open Circulatory System Closed Circulatory System
    Blood Flow Mechanism Blood pumped by heart passes through large vessels into open body cavities/spaces called sinuses. Blood pumped by heart is always circulated through a closed network of blood vessels.
    Regulation Precision Low flow regulation. More advantageous: fluid flow can be precisely regulated.
    Animal Examples Arthropods and Molluscs Annelids and Chordates (All Vertebrates)

    2. Evolutionary Changes in Vertebrate Hearts

    Animal Group Chamber Count Heart Anatomy Circulation Type Blood Flow Path & Details
    Fishes 2 Chambers 1 Atrium + 1 Ventricle Single Circulation Heart pumps deoxygenated blood → oxygenated by gills → body parts → deoxygenated blood returns to heart.
    Amphibians & Reptiles
    (Except Crocodiles)    		 3 Chambers 2 Atria + 1 Ventricle Incomplete Double Circulation Left atrium gets oxygenated blood (gills/lungs/skin); Right atrium gets deoxygenated blood. Blood mixes in single ventricle before pumping out.
    Crocodiles, Birds & Mammals 4 Chambers 2 Atria + 2 Ventricles Complete Double Circulation Oxygenated and deoxygenated blood received by Left & Right atria pass to respective ventricles without any mixing. Two separate pathways.
    15.3.1

    HUMAN HEART ANATOMY & NODAL TISSUE

    NCERT Page 198–199

    Anatomical Overview & Location

    • Embryonic Origin: Mesodermally derived organ.
    • Position: Situated in thoracic cavity, between two lungs, slightly tilted to left.
    • Size: Approximately size of a clenched fist.
    • Protection: Enclosed by a double-walled membranous bag, pericardium, enclosing pericardial fluid.

    Chambers, Septa & Heart Valves

    • Upper Chambers: 2 small upper chambers called Atria (Right & Left).
    • Lower Chambers: 2 larger lower chambers called Ventricles (Right & Left). Thick ventricular walls.
    • Inter-atrial Septum: Thin muscular wall separating Right & Left atria.
    • Inter-ventricular Septum: Thick wall separating Right & Left ventricles.
    • Tricuspid Valve: 3 muscular flaps guarding opening between Right Atrium & Right Ventricle.
    • Bicuspid / Mitral Valve: Guards opening between Left Atrium & Left Ventricle.
    • Semilunar Valves: Guard openings of Right & Left ventricles into Pulmonary Artery and Aorta.
    • Valve Function: Ensures strictly unidirectional flow (Atria → Ventricles → Arteries), preventing backflow.

    Nodal Tissue & Auto-excitability

    Myogenic Heart

    Specialised cardiac musculature called nodal tissue is distributed throughout the heart. It is autoexcitable (generates action potentials without external neural stimuli).

    1. Sino-Atrial Node (SAN):
    Located in right upper corner of right atrium. Generates maximum action potentials: 70–75 $\text{min}^{-1}$. Initiates and maintains rhythmic contractile activity. Called the PACEMAKER of the heart.
    2. Atrio-Ventricular Node (AVN):
    Located in lower left corner of right atrium close to atrio-ventricular septum.
    3. AV Bundle & Bundle of His:
    Nodal fibres continuing from AVN, passing through AV septa, dividing at top of interventricular septum into Right & Left bundle branches.
    4. Purkinje Fibres:
    Minute conductive fibres distributed throughout ventricular musculature. Bundle branches + Purkinje fibres form Bundle of His.
    NCERT Figure 15.2 — Diagrammatic Sectional View of Human Heart
    ⚡ Sequence of Cardiac Action Potential Impulse Propagation
    1 SAN (Sino-Atrial Node): Generates impulse (70-75/min) → Atrial Contraction
    2 AVN (Atrio-Ventricular Node): Receives wave, slightly delays for ventricular filling
    3 AV Bundle / Bundle of His: Transmits wave through interventricular septum
    4 Purkinje Fibres: Spreads impulse across entire ventricular myocardium → Ventricular Systole
    15.3.2

    CARDIAC CYCLE, STROKE VOLUME & HEART SOUNDS

    NCERT Page 199–200
    Stage 1

    Joint Diastole

    All 4 chambers in relaxed state. Tricuspid and bicuspid valves are open. Blood from pulmonary veins & vena cava flows into left and right ventricles through atria. Semilunar valves are closed.

    Stage 2

    Atrial Systole

    SAN generates action potential. Both atria contract simultaneously. Increases blood flow into ventricles by about 30%.

    Stage 3

    Ventricular Systole

    Impulse transmitted via AVN & Bundle of His. Ventricles contract, atria relax. Ventricular pressure increases → Closure of AV valves (produces LUB sound) → Semilunar valves forced open → Blood pumped into aorta & pulmonary artery.

    Stage 4

    Ventricular Diastole

    Ventricles relax. Ventricular pressure falls → Closure of semilunar valves (produces DUB sound). Pressure drops further → AV valves open → Return to Joint Diastole.

    📐 Quantitative Cardiac Parameters (NCERT Formulas)

    CYCLE DURATION 0.8 Seconds

    Calculated as $60 \text{ sec} / 72 \text{ beats}$.

    STROKE VOLUME (SV) ~70 mL

    Volume of blood pumped out by each ventricle per beat.

    CARDIAC OUTPUT (CO) ~5000 mL (5 Litres)

    $\text{CO} = \text{Stroke Volume} \times \text{Heart Rate}$

    NCERT Fact: The body can alter both stroke volume and heart rate. For example, the cardiac output of an athlete is much higher than that of an ordinary man.

    Heart Sounds (Clinical Diagnostic Significance)

    Heart Sound Pitch & Duration Cause / Valve Associated Timing in Cardiac Cycle
    First Sound: 'LUB' Lower pitch, longer duration Closure of Tricuspid and Bicuspid (AV) valves Marks beginning of Ventricular Systole
    Second Sound: 'DUB' Higher pitch, shorter duration Closure of Semilunar valves Marks beginning of Ventricular Diastole
    15.3.3

    ELECTROCARDIOGRAM (ECG)

    NCERT Page 200–201
    ⚠️ Terminology Distinction:
    • Electro-cardiograph: The machine used to obtain the trace.
    • Electrocardiogram (ECG): The graphical representation of the electrical activity of the heart.

    Standard ECG Lead Configurations

    • Standard ECG Setup: Patient is connected to machine with 3 electrical leads:
      • One to each wrist (Left & Right)
      • One to the left ankle
    • Detailed Evaluation: Requires multiple leads attached to the chest region.
    • Heart Rate Calculation: By counting the number of QRS complexes in a given time period, one can determine the heart rate of an individual.

    ECG Wave Analysis

    ECG Peak Electrical Event Represented Mechanical Event / Clinical Note
    P-Wave Electrical excitation (Depolarisation) of atria. Leads to contraction of both atria.
    QRS Complex Depolarisation of ventricles. Initiates ventricular contraction; starts shortly after Q; marks beginning of systole.
    T-Wave Return of ventricles from excited to normal state (Repolarisation). End of T-wave marks the end of ventricular systole.
    NCERT Figure 15.3 — Diagrammatic Presentation of a Standard ECG Waveform
    *Any deviation from standard shape indicates potential cardiac abnormality or disease, giving ECG immense clinical significance.
    15.4

    DOUBLE CIRCULATION & BLOOD VESSEL HISTOLOGY

    NCERT Page 201–202

    1. Histological Structure of Blood Vessels (Arteries & Veins)

    Each artery and vein consists of three distinct layers (tunics):

    Layer Name Tissue Composition Artery vs Vein Structural Difference
    Tunica Intima (Inner Layer) Inner lining of squamous endothelium. Identical lining in both.
    Tunica Media (Middle Layer) Smooth muscle cells and elastic fibres. Comparatively thin in Veins; thick & muscular in Arteries.
    Tunica Externa (Outer Layer) Fibrous connective tissue with collagen fibres. Protective outer coat in both.

    Pulmonary vs Systemic Circulation

    1. Pulmonary Circulation

    Right Ventricle (Deox Blood) → Pulmonary Artery → Lungs (Oxygenation) → Pulmonary Veins → Left Atrium.

    2. Systemic Circulation

    Left Ventricle (Ox Blood) → Aorta → Arteries/Capillaries → Tissues → Vena Cava (Deox Blood) → Right Atrium. Provides $O_2$ & nutrients; removes $CO_2$.

    Special Circulation Systems

    ⭐ HEPATIC PORTAL SYSTEM

    A unique vascular connection existing between the digestive tract and liver. The hepatic portal vein carries blood from intestine to liver before delivering it to systemic circulation.

    ⭐ CORONARY SYSTEM

    A special system of blood vessels present exclusively for the circulation of blood to and from the cardiac musculature itself.

    15.5

    REGULATION OF CARDIAC ACTIVITY

    NCERT Page 202
    1. Intrinsic / Myogenic Control

    Nodal Auto-Regulation

    Normal activities of the human heart are regulated intrinsically (auto-regulated) by specialized muscles (nodal tissue). Hence, the human heart is termed MYOGENIC.

    2. Neural Regulation (Medulla Oblongata)

    Autonomic Nervous System (ANS)

    • Sympathetic Nerves: Increase heart beat rate, increase strength of ventricular contraction, and thereby increase cardiac output.
    • Parasympathetic Nerves: Decrease heart beat rate, decrease speed of conduction of action potential, and thereby decrease cardiac output.
    3. Hormonal Regulation

    Adrenal Medullary Hormones

    Hormones secreted by adrenal medulla (Adrenaline / Noradrenaline) increase cardiac output during stress / emergency responses.

    15.6

    DISORDERS OF CIRCULATORY SYSTEM

    NCERT Page 202–203

    1. High Blood Pressure (Hypertension)

    > 140/90 mmHg
    • Normal BP: $120/80 \text{ mm Hg}$ ($120 =$ Systolic/pumping pressure, $80 =$ Diastolic/resting pressure).
    • Hypertension Diagnosis: Repeated checks showing 140/90 or higher.
    • Effects: Leads to heart diseases; affects vital organs like brain and kidney.

    2. Coronary Artery Disease (CAD / Atherosclerosis)

    Narrowing Lumen
    • Affects blood vessels supplying blood to heart muscle (coronary arteries).
    • Cause: Deposits of calcium, fat, cholesterol and fibrous tissues.
    • Result: Makes the lumen of arteries narrower, restricting blood flow.

    3. Angina (Angina Pectoris)

    Acute Chest Pain
    • Symptom: Acute chest pain when not enough oxygen reaches heart muscle.
    • Occurs in men/women of any age, but more common in middle-aged and elderly.

    4. Heart Failure

    Congestive State
    • State of heart when it is not pumping blood effectively enough to meet body needs.
    • Called congestive heart failure because lung congestion is a main symptom.

    ⭐ Key Diagnostic Distinctions (NCERT Highlight)

    Condition Medical Definition & Core Pathology
    Heart Failure Heart is not pumping blood effectively enough to meet body requirements.
    Cardiac Arrest State when the heart completely stops beating.
    Heart Attack (Myocardial Infarction) State when heart muscle is suddenly damaged by an inadequate blood supply.

    TEXTBOOK EXERCISES & MASTER SOLUTIONS

    NCERT Page 204

    Q1. Name the components of the formed elements in the blood and mention one major function of each of them.

    Ans: 1. Erythrocytes (RBCs): Transport respiratory gases ($O_2$ and $CO_2$).
    2. Leucocytes (WBCs): Immune defense mechanisms against foreign pathogens.
    3. Thrombocytes (Platelets): Blood coagulation/clotting.

    Q2. What is the importance of plasma proteins?

    Ans: Plasma proteins contribute 6-8% of plasma. Major proteins include: Fibrinogen (essential for blood clotting), Globulins (involved in defense mechanisms/antibodies), and Albumins (maintain osmotic balance).

    Q3. Match Column I with Column II:

    Column IColumn II (Matched Solution)
    (a) Eosinophils(iii) Resist Infections
    (b) RBC(v) Gas transport
    (c) AB Group(ii) Universal Recipient
    (d) Platelets(i) Coagulation
    (e) Systole(iv) Contraction of Heart

    Q4. Why do we consider blood as a connective tissue?

    Ans: Blood is derived mesodermally and connects/links all tissues and organs of the body by transporting nutrients, oxygen, hormones, and removing wastes throughout the liquid matrix (plasma).

    Q5. What is the difference between lymph and blood?

    Ans: Blood contains RBCs, high proteins, and platelets, whereas Lymph is colourless, lacks RBCs/platelets, has lower protein content, but is rich in specialized lymphocytes.

    Q6. What is meant by double circulation? What is its significance?

    Ans: Blood passes through the heart twice during one complete cycle (Pulmonary + Systemic circulation). Its significance is that it prevents mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood, ensuring highly efficient oxygen delivery to body tissues.

    Q7. Write the differences between: (a) Open & Closed system (b) Systole & Diastole (c) P-wave & T-wave

    Ans: (a) Open system circulates blood into open sinuses, closed system circulates through closed vessels.
    (b) Systole refers to contraction phase of heart chambers; Diastole refers to relaxation phase.
    (c) P-wave represents atrial depolarisation; T-wave represents ventricular repolarisation.

    Q8–Q14 Answers Quick Reference:

    • Q8 (Heart Evolution): Fishes (2-chambered, single) → Amphibians/Reptiles (3-chambered, incomplete double) → Birds/Mammals (4-chambered, complete double).
    • Q9 (Myogenic Heart): Heart contraction is initiated intrinsically by specialized nodal muscular tissue without external nervous stimulation.
    • Q10 (SAN as Pacemaker): Generates maximum rate of action potentials (70-75/min) setting the rhythm for the entire heart.
    • Q11 (AVN & AV Bundle): Conducts action potential generated by SAN down through interventricular septum to Purkinje fibres for ventricular contraction.
    • Q12 (Cardiac Cycle & Output): Sequential repeating events of contraction and relaxation in a heart beat (0.8s). Cardiac Output = Stroke Volume (70 mL) × Heart Rate (72/min) = ~5 Litres/min.
    • Q13 (Heart Sounds): Lub (closure of AV valves during ventricular systole) and Dub (closure of semilunar valves during ventricular diastole).
