18.1 Coordination & Neural System

NCERT Page 230-231
📖 Definition: Coordination

Coordination is the physiological process through which two or more organs interact and complement the functions of one another to maintain Homeostasis (steady internal state).

Exercise Example (Integrated Physiology):

  • Increased Physical Activity → Higher muscular energy demand → Increased $O_2$ requirement.
  • Systemic Response: Increased rate of respiration + Increased heart beat + Increased blood flow via blood vessels.
  • Post-Exercise Recovery: Nerves, lungs, heart, and kidneys gradually return to normal resting states in a synchronized fashion.
⚡ Neural System

Provides an organized network of point-to-point connections for rapid / quick coordination.

🧪 Endocrine System

Provides chemical integration through hormones (slower, long-lasting regulation).

Comparative Neural Organization across Animal Kingdom:
  • Lower Invertebrates (e.g., Hydra): Composed of a simple network of neurons.
  • Insects: Better organized; possess a brain along with a number of ganglia and neural tissues.
  • Vertebrates: Possess a highly developed and complex neural system.

18.2 Human Neural System Structure

NCERT Page 231
HUMAN NEURAL SYSTEM
1. Central Neural System (CNS)

Includes Brain + Spinal Cord. Primary site of information processing and control.

2. Peripheral Neural System (PNS)

All nerves of body associated with CNS.

PNS Fibre Type Direction of Impulse Flow Functional Target
Afferent Fibres (Sensory) Tissues / Organs → CNS Transmits sensory inputs to processing center
Efferent Fibres (Motor) CNS → Peripheral Tissues / Organs Transmits regulatory motor commands to effectors
Somatic Neural System (SNS)

Relays impulses from CNS to skeletal muscles (Voluntary movement control).

Autonomic Neural System (ANS)

Transmits impulses from CNS to involuntary organs & smooth muscles.

Subdivisions: (i) Sympathetic & (ii) Parasympathetic
Visceral Nervous System: Part of PNS comprising nerves, fibres, ganglia, and plexuses by which impulses travel between CNS and the viscera (internal organs).

18.3 Neuron — Structural and Functional Unit

NCERT Pages 231-232
Figure 18.1 — Detailed Structure of a Neuron
1. Cell Body (Soma)

Contains cytoplasm with typical cell organelles and granular bodies called Nissl's granules.

2. Dendrites

Short, repeatedly branched fibres projecting out of cell body. Contain Nissl's granules. Transmit impulses towards cell body (centripetal).

3. Axon

Long single fibre with branched distal end. Terminates in bulb-like Synaptic Knobs containing neurotransmitters. Transmits impulses away from cell body (centrifugal).

Structural Types of Neurons (Based on number of Axons & Dendrites)
Neuron Type Structural Composition Specific NCERT Anatomical Location
Multipolar One Axon + Two or more Dendrites Cerebral Cortex
Bipolar One Axon + One Dendrite Retina of Eye
Unipolar Cell body with One Axon only Usually found in Embryonic Stage
⚡ Myelinated Nerve Fibres
  • Enveloped with Schwann cells that form a myelin sheath around the axon.
  • Gaps between adjacent sheaths = Nodes of Ranvier.
  • Found in: Spinal and Cranial Nerves.
🔌 Unmyelinated Nerve Fibres
  • Enclosed by Schwann cells that DO NOT form a myelin sheath around the axon.
  • No Nodes of Ranvier.
  • Found in: Autonomous and Somatic neural systems.

18.3.1 Generation and Conduction of Nerve Impulse

NCERT Pages 232-233
🧮 Mathematical & Ion Gradient Mechanics of Axonal Membrane Physiological Constants
1. Active Transport Pump Ratio:

Sodium-Potassium Pump ($\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$ ATPase)

Transports: 3 $\text{Na}^+$ OUTWARDS for 2 $\text{K}^+$ INWARDS (Uses 1 ATP)

2. Resting Potential Equation:

Resting Membrane Potential $\approx -70\text{ mV}$

Inner surface: Negative (-) | Outer surface: Positive (+)

3. Action Potential Equation:

Action Potential (Nerve Impulse) $\approx +30\text{ mV}$

Polartiy Reversal at Site A: Inner (+) | Outer (-)

STAGE 1: Resting State (Polarised Membrane)

  • 1 Selective Permeability: Axonal membrane is comparatively more permeable to $\text{K}^+$, nearly impermeable to $\text{Na}^+$, and completely impermeable to negatively charged proteins in axoplasm.
  • 2 Concentration Gradient established:
    • Inside Axoplasm: High $[\text{K}^+]$ + High negatively charged proteins + Low $[\text{Na}^+]$.
    • Outside ECF: High $[\text{Na}^+]$ + Low $[\text{K}^+]$.
  • 3 Active Maintenance: Maintained by active transport via $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$ pump ($3\text{Na}^+$ out / $2\text{K}^+$ in). Outer surface positive (+), inner negative (-). Membrane is Polarised.

STAGE 2: Depolarisation & Circuit Propagation

  • 1 Stimulus Application (at Site A): Axonal membrane becomes freely permeable to $\text{Na}^+$, causing rapid influx of $\text{Na}^+$.
  • 2 Reversal of Polarity: Inner side becomes (+), outer becomes (-). Membrane at Site A is Depolarised. The electrical potential difference is the Action Potential (Nerve Impulse).
  • 3 Current Circuit Flow:
    • Inner surface: Current flows from Site A → Site B.
    • Outer surface: Current flows from Site B → Site A (completes circuit).
    • Depolarisation shifts to Site B; sequence repeats along axon length.
  • 4 Repolarisation: $\text{Na}^+$ permeability is extremely short-lived, followed by immediate rise in $\text{K}^+$ permeability. $\text{K}^+$ diffuses outside, restoring resting potential.

18.3.2 Transmission of Impulses across Synapse

NCERT Pages 234-235
Figure 18.3 — Axon Terminal and Chemical Synapse
Feature Electrical Synapse Chemical Synapse
Synaptic Cleft / Gap Pre- & post-synaptic membranes in very close proximity (No functional fluid gap). Separated by a fluid-filled space called Synaptic Cleft.
Mode of Flow Electrical current flows directly across gap junctions. Requires chemical messengers called Neurotransmitters.
Transmission Speed Always faster (resembles conduction along single axon). Relatively slower due to neurotransmitter release & binding delay.
Abundance in Humans Rare in the human neural system. Predominant type in human nervous system.
🔄 Sequential Mechanism of Chemical Synaptic Transmission
Step 1 Action potential arrives at the axon terminal.
Step 2 Stimulates movement & fusion of synaptic vesicles with pre-synaptic membrane.
Step 3 Release of neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft via exocytosis.
Step 4 Neurotransmitters bind to specific receptors on post-synaptic membrane.
Step 5 Opens ion channels → Ion entry generates New Potential (Excitatory or Inhibitory).

18.4 Central Neural System — Human Brain Anatomy & Functions

NCERT Pages 235-236
🧠 Brain as Command & Control Center

Controls voluntary movements, body balance, functioning of vital involuntary organs (lungs, heart, kidneys), thermoregulation, hunger & thirst, circadian (24-hour) rhythms, endocrine activities, and human behavior. Processing site for vision, hearing, speech, memory, intelligence, emotions, and thoughts.

🛡️ Cranial Meninges (3 Protective Layers)
  1. Dura mater: Outer layer (tough & fibrous).
  2. Arachnoid: Very thin middle layer.
  3. Pia mater: Inner layer in direct contact with brain tissue.
Figure 18.4 — Sagittal Section of the Human Brain
1. FOREBRAIN (Cerebrum + Thalamus + Hypothalamus)
  • Cerebrum: Major part of brain. Longitudinally cleft into Left & Right Cerebral Hemispheres, connected by Corpus Callosum (tract of nerve fibres).
  • Cerebral Cortex (Grey Matter): Thrown into prominent folds. Cell bodies concentrated here. Contains:
    • Motor Areas | Sensory Areas
    • Association Areas: Large regions neither clearly sensory nor motor. Responsible for intersensory associations, memory, and communication.
  • White Matter: Inner cerebral hemisphere made of myelinated nerve fibre tracts.
  • Thalamus: Major coordinating center for sensory and motor signaling.
  • Hypothalamus: At base of thalamus. Controls body temperature, urge for eating & drinking. Contains neurosecretory cells secreting hypothalamic hormones.
  • Limbic System / Limbic Lobe: Inner cerebral hemispheres + Amygdala + Hippocampus. Regulates sexual behavior, emotional reactions (excitement, pleasure, rage, fear), and motivation.
2. MIDBRAIN

Located between forebrain (thalamus/hypothalamus) and hindbrain (pons).

• Passageway: Cerebral Aqueduct canal passes through it.

• Dorsal portion: 4 round swellings/lobes called Corpora Quadrigemina.

3. HINDBRAIN

• Pons: Fibre tracts interconnecting different brain regions.

• Cerebellum: Very convoluted surface providing extra space for many more neurons.

• Medulla Oblongata: Connected to spinal cord. Controls respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, and gastric secretions.

🧠 Brain Stem Components: Midbrain + Pons + Medulla Oblongata

📝 Section 9: Complete NCERT Chapter Exercises & Official Solutions

All 10 Questions Solved
Q1. Briefly describe the structure of the Brain.

Answer: The human brain is protected by the skull and covered by three cranial meninges: outer dura mater, middle arachnoid, and inner pia mater. It is divided into three major parts:
1. Forebrain: Consists of Cerebrum (left & right hemispheres connected by corpus callosum; cortex contains motor, sensory & association areas), Thalamus (relay center), and Hypothalamus (thermoregulation, hunger, thirst, hormone secretion). Limbic system controls emotions/motivation.
2. Midbrain: Located between forebrain and hindbrain; contains cerebral aqueduct and corpora quadrigemina (4 optic/auditory lobes).
3. Hindbrain: Consists of Pons (fibre tracts), Cerebellum (convoluted, controls muscular balance), and Medulla Oblongata (controls respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, gastric secretions).

Q2. Compare the following: (a) Central neural system (CNS) and Peripheral neural system (PNS) (b) Resting potential and action potential

Answer:
(a) CNS vs PNS: CNS comprises brain and spinal cord and acts as the central site for information processing and control. PNS comprises all nerves associated with CNS, categorized into afferent/efferent and somatic/autonomic divisions.
(b) Resting vs Action Potential: Resting potential is the electrical potential (-70mV) across an unstimulated polarised axonal membrane (outer +, inner -). Action potential (+30mV) is the temporary reversal of polarity (outer -, inner +) caused by rapid $\text{Na}^+$ influx upon stimulus application.

Q3. Explain the following processes: (a) Polarisation of the membrane of a nerve fibre (b) Depolarisation of the membrane of a nerve fibre (c) Transmission of a nerve impulse across a chemical synapse

Answer:
(a) Polarisation: In resting state, axonal membrane is more permeable to $\text{K}^+$ and impermeable to $\text{Na}^+$ and negative proteins. $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$ pump transports $3\text{Na}^+$ out for $2\text{K}^+$ in, creating a positive outer surface and negative inner surface (-70mV).
(b) Depolarisation: Stimulus causes sudden increase in $\text{Na}^+$ permeability. Influx of $\text{Na}^+$ reverses polarity at that site (inner +, outer -), generating an action potential.
(c) Synaptic Transmission: Action potential arriving at axon terminal causes synaptic vesicles to fuse with pre-synaptic membrane, releasing neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft. They bind to post-synaptic receptors, opening ion channels to generate a new potential.

Q5. Write short notes on the following: (a) Neural coordination (b) Forebrain (c) Midbrain (d) Hindbrain (e) Synapse

Answer:
(a) Neural coordination: Rapid process of interaction between organs to complement functions and maintain homeostasis via organized point-to-point connections.
(b) Forebrain: Consists of cerebrum, thalamus, and hypothalamus; controls higher cognitive functions, emotions, body temp, and hunger.
(c) Midbrain: Integrates visual, tactile, and auditory inputs; contains corpora quadrigemina and cerebral aqueduct.
(d) Hindbrain: Comprises pons, cerebellum, and medulla; regulates balance, cardiovascular reflexes, gastric secretions, and respiration.
(e) Synapse: Functional junction between pre- and post-synaptic neurons (electrical or chemical via synaptic cleft).

Q6. Give a brief account of Mechanism of synaptic transmission.

Answer: Arriving action potential → Synaptic vesicle translocation → Fusion with pre-synaptic membrane → Exocytosis of neurotransmitters into synaptic cleft → Binding to post-synaptic receptors → Ion channel opening → Generation of excitatory/inhibitory potential.

Q7. Explain the role of $\text{Na}^+$ in the generation of action potential.

Answer: Upon stimulation, the axonal membrane becomes freely permeable to $\text{Na}^+$. The rapid influx of $\text{Na}^+$ down its concentration gradient neutralizes negative internal charges and turns the axoplasm positive relative to the outside, generating the action potential.

Q9. Answer the following: (a) Which part of the human brain is the most developed? (b) Which part of our central neural system acts as a master clock?

Answer:
(a) The Cerebrum (specifically the Cerebral Cortex) is the most developed part of the human brain.
(b) The Hypothalamus (specifically the Suprachiasmatic nucleus) acts as the master clock controlling 24-hour circadian rhythms.

Q10. Distinguish between: (a) afferent neurons and efferent neurons (b) impulse conduction in a myelinated nerve fibre and unmyelinated nerve fibre (c) cranial nerves and spinal nerves

Answer:
(a) Afferent vs Efferent: Afferent carries sensory impulses from organs to CNS; Efferent carries motor regulatory commands from CNS to effectors.
(b) Myelinated vs Unmyelinated Conduction: Myelinated uses saltatory conduction (fast jumping between Nodes of Ranvier); Unmyelinated uses continuous wave conduction (slower).
(c) Cranial vs Spinal Nerves: Cranial nerves originate directly from the brain (12 pairs in humans); Spinal nerves originate from the spinal cord (31 pairs).
